Your gallery passes are not timed — they're good for the whole Congress

Paper Senate gallery passes are valid for the entire two-year Congress — a rule since 1981. Not date-stamped, not timed, and reusable. They are not transferable, so each person needs their own, but Mary's passes work on any day the gallery is open through the end of 2026.

What this changes:

  • You can go more than once. Duck in Wednesday after the markup, then go back Thursday when the votes are.
  • Re-entry is fine. Leave when it goes quiet, come back when it doesn't. Re-checking bags and phones is the only friction.
  • Thursday is de-risked. If votes land at an awkward hour, the pass doesn't expire.

The real constraint is the Senate, not the pass. The gallery opens 30 minutes before the Senate convenes and closes when it adjourns. Which is why 202-224-0057 matters more than the ticket in your pocket.

One exception: the e-Gallery QR pass, if you booked one as backup, is date-and-time specific. The paper pass is the flexible one.

Why a markup beats a hearing, and what you'll actually see

A hearing is witnesses and speeches. A markup is the committee working on the bill itself — amendments offered, argued, withdrawn, voted. It is the closest thing to watching legislation being written that exists in public.

Expect it to look chaotic and sound procedural. Members drift in and out. The chair moves fast. Somebody will say "I'll withdraw my amendment" after a quiet conversation at the side, and that conversation was the actual event.

Supreme Court
Correction — the Courtroom lecture is off, and it's not a maybe

I had this as a highlight and it was wrong. The Courtroom is closed from Friday 1 August to Friday 29 August — the whole of your visit — and no courtroom lectures take place during that window. August dates aren't even bookable.

The counter-intuitive part is that lectures normally run because the Court isn't sitting — the Courtroom is free, so docents use it. Summer is usually the best time for them. This particular August is the exception.

What's still worth the forty minutes: the marble Great Hall with its busts of every Chief Justice, the ground-floor exhibitions on the Court's history, the visitor film, and the spiral staircases. Free, three minutes from the Capitol, and quiet. Just go in knowing you'll not see the bench.

Confirm on the day at supremecourt.gov or by checking "Today at the Court" on their homepage — and if the building itself is shut for the same maintenance, give the time to the Senate gallery instead.

If Ryan and Violet would rather not — two things, both beside the Library

A funding-bill roll call is Mary's idea of a good afternoon and possibly nobody else's. These two are a four-minute walk from the Capitol and a two-minute walk from the Library of Congress steps, so the 15:45 reconvene is trivial. You can do both in ninety minutes.

Folger Shakespeare Library — the pick. Reopened in 2024 after a four-year rebuild, and for the first time all 82 of its First Folios are displayed together: the largest collection on earth, and the book that preserved eighteen plays — Macbeth and Julius Caesar among them — that would otherwise have been lost. There's an Elizabethan theatre and new gardens too. Free, $15 suggested, and reserve a timed pass for the exhibition halls. folger.edu

United States Botanic Garden — the no-booking backup, three minutes from the Capitol's west front. Free, no ticket, open 10 to 5. The Jungle room sits under a 93-foot glass dome with a canopy walkway running through the treetops, which is both the coldest and the most photogenic ten minutes on Capitol Hill. Half an hour is plenty. usbg.gov

National Gallery of Art — the low-effort one, and the pure break. Twelve minutes west along the Mall, and the underground concourse joining the two buildings is Multiverse: 41,000 programmed LEDs running 200 feet overhead, never repeating the same pattern twice. You walk through it rather than at it. Free, no ticket, open till 17:00, and it asks nothing of you after a morning of marble and procedure. Twenty minutes if you're only there for the tunnel; an hour if the East Building's Calder mobile pulls you upstairs.

Still on the table: the Helsinki Commission briefing at 14:00 in RHOB-2247 on wartime Ukraine, if a second committee room appeals more than a first folio.

Or save it for Friday. The concourse entrance is a six-minute walk from Mitsitam, so a 20-minute detour after lunch works too — worth it on the hottest day of the trip, though it does eat the front of the hotel rest block.

What to watch for from the gallery — four things that look like nothing

Cloture versus passage. Cloture ends debate and needs 60; passage needs 51. The Senate votes twice on the same bill and the first vote is the real one.

"Without objection, so ordered." Most Senate business happens by unanimous consent — a clerk reading an agreement nobody objects to. Hear that phrase and you just watched the Senate legislate.

The well. During a roll call, senators gather at the front, and that is where the persuasion happens. A fifteen-minute vote routinely runs forty-five because of it.

An empty chamber is normal, not broken. Members are in committee or negotiating. They come for votes.

Turbo 3.0 is in the catalogue — and it's offsite. Here's what that actually means

The record reads:

Status  Available
Location  Science & Business Reading Room Only (Adams, 5th Floor)
         Offsite
Call no.  TL215.P75 S575 2018 CABIN BRANCH SpecMat

Decoded, line by line:

  • TL215.P75 — Technology, motor vehicles, Porsche. Exactly where a marque history belongs, and S575 is the Cutter number for Snodgrass. That call number is yours.
  • Science & Business Reading Room Only — it does not circulate to the Main Reading Room. It can only be consulted in that specific room, on the 5th floor of the John Adams Building, which is a different building from the Jefferson one your timed entry is for.
  • Offsite · CABIN BRANCH — the volume is not on Capitol Hill at all. Cabin Branch is the Library's off-site store. Retrieval is measured in business days, not hours.
  • SpecMat — special materials handling, which adds another approval step.

The honest verdict: you cannot hold this copy on Thursday. Offsite plus a restricted reading room plus special-materials handling is at minimum a next-business-day retrieval, and you fly home before that lands. No amount of turning up early fixes it.

The one call worth making tomorrow. Ring the Library on 202-707-5000 and ask for the Science, Technology & Business Division, or write via ask.loc.gov. Tell them plainly: you are the author, you are in Washington Thursday only, and you would like to know whether an advance recall from Cabin Branch to the Adams reading room is possible for that afternoon. Sometimes it is. Librarians are quite good to authors who ask nicely, and the worst outcome is a no you already have.

Check Carrera 2.7 separately. You've only pulled one record. The 2015 title may sit in the general collections rather than Science & Business, and if it is on-site it behaves completely differently — that one could come to a reading room the same day.

Carrera 2.7 is onsite. This one you can actually hold.

General Collections · Onsite — which is a completely different animal from Turbo 3.0. Onsite means it lives in the closed stacks under Capitol Hill, and a registered reader can have it carried up to a desk in the Main Reading Room. Same day. Under the dome.

The obstacle is only clock arithmetic. Registration takes about 20 minutes, retrieval runs 45 to 90, and you don't reach the Library until 15:45 on a day it closes at 17:00. Placing the request when you arrive is too late. So place it earlier and let the book be waiting.

Route A — Wednesday morning, and it costs you almost nothing. Reader registration is at the Madison Building, room LM 133, open Tuesday to Saturday 8:30 to noon. Madison is a ten-minute walk from Russell, where you already need to be by 9:25 for the markup.

  • 8:30 Madison LM 133 — photo ID, register, free card, valid two years
  • 8:55 place the request for Carrera 2.7, delivery to the Main Reading Room, for Thursday
  • 9:10 walk to Russell — you are back on schedule with the morning untouched

Route B — Thursday, trading the Foreign Relations hearing. Skip the 11:20 hearing, walk to Madison instead, register before the noon cut-off and request the book immediately. It will be on the desk by mid-afternoon, waiting when you arrive at 15:45. You lose a committee room and gain your own book.

Before either: check whether the Library now lets you pre-register online and simply collect the card at the desk — it would cut the Wednesday stop to five minutes. Worth two minutes on loc.gov tonight.

Then, on Thursday: your own book, carried up out of the national collection by a stranger who has no idea you wrote it, placed on a desk beneath the dome of the Main Reading Room. Violet should take that photograph. Nothing else on this itinerary comes close.

The other two ISBNs, and how to read any record you pull

Both Parabolica Press titles should be held. Search catalog.loc.gov by ISBN:

  • Carrera 2.7 — 9780996268288
  • Turbo 3.0, Limited Edition — 9780996268240
  • Turbo 3.0, Publisher's Edition — 9780996268264

Or search author Snodgrass, Ryan. Automotive marque histories classify around TL215. What you want off each record is the LCCN (control number), the call number, and — the one that decides everything — the holding location.

Do this tonight, not on the day. If the record says the volume lives on-site, retrieval to the Main Reading Room is possible. If it says Fort Meade — the off-site storage where most low-circulation books sleep — retrieval takes 24 to 72 hours and cannot happen during this trip. Better to know now.

What to do instead on Thursday — three things that do work

Given Turbo 3.0 is offsite, aim at these instead — in descending order of how good the moment is.

  • A free Reader Identification Card — photo ID, about twenty minutes, anyone sixteen or older
  • An on-site holding, not Fort Meade
  • Roughly 45 to 90 minutes for delivery after you place the request

The problem: you arrive at 15:45 and the building closes at 17:00. Registration plus retrieval does not fit inside 75 minutes.

What to do instead. Ask at the desk whether the Jefferson Building's registration station (room LJ 139) is open that afternoon. If it is, get the card anyway — it's valid two years, it takes twenty minutes, and it makes the Main Reading Room something you can walk into and sit down in rather than look at from a balcony. Place the request before you leave; if the timing collapses, you've still stood in the room as a registered reader rather than a tourist.

The move that always works: pull the catalogue record up on your phone standing in the Great Hall. Your own book, its own control number, in the catalogue of the Library of Congress. Show Violet. That is the moment, and it needs no card, no request slip and no waiting.

If you want certainty: email the reference desk through ask.loc.gov tomorrow — ask where those three ISBNs are held and whether same-day retrieval is possible on Thursday afternoon. They answer, and they are good at this.

AS 3 to Seattle

Five hours forty in the air, landing 7:59 pm Pacific. Your seats are on the left side — A is the window.

Look out of the window for the first two minutes

DCA has the most beautiful departure in American aviation. Noise rules over the capital mean aircraft follow the Potomac rather than climbing straight out, so you leave the city by tracing the river through the middle of it.

On the usual northbound departure the aircraft banks left over Georgetown, which drops your wing and opens up the Kennedy Center, Roosevelt Island, Georgetown and the university Violet toured on Saturday. The Mall and the Capitol sit on the opposite side. Which you get depends on the runway in use that afternoon, so it can just as easily be reversed.

Either way: phones ready about ninety seconds after wheels-up. Better last look at Washington than any observation deck.

Soy — sort this at Union Market, not at the airport

This flight leaves at dinnertime and lands near eleven, Washington time. Alaska's main cabin is buy-on-board with no allergy-safe meal service, and soy lecithin is in most packaged snacks, breads, chocolate and sauces — so treat the onboard trolley as unavailable rather than as a fallback.

Union Market is the right place to solve it, and it's already in your morning. You can read labels, and you can ask vendors directly rather than guessing at a sealed packet. Buy Ryan's dinner there at 12:30 and carry it on.

What travels well and is usually soy-free

Whole fruit, hard cheese, cured meat, plain nuts, olives, rice crackers, crisps fried in olive or sunflower oil, and a proper sandwich from a counter that can tell you what's in the bread. Bread and chocolate are the usual ambushes — both carry soy lecithin more often than not.

Through security: solid food is fine in any quantity. Anything spoonable — yogurt, hummus, dips — counts as a liquid and is capped at 3.4 oz. Empty water bottles through, fill airside.

Antihistamines and anything prescribed go in the carry-on, not the checked bag. If a reaction starts at 35,000 feet, the hold is not reachable.

None of this is medical advice — it's just the logistics of a five-and-a-half hour flight where the galley can't help.

Floor briefing

Mary · researched Mon 3 Aug

Enough to follow a government funding fight in real time, from the gallery.

The bill — a continuing resolution through December 11, and why it's technically an Africa trade bill

H.R. 6500 funds the government and averts a shutdown before the midterms. Cloture cleared 89–4 on Monday — overwhelming and bipartisan, which means this passes rather than blows up. Recess is targeted around 10 August.

Murray's own framing: a "much-improved CR" that adds extensions the House version omitted, rejects the administration's war-spending request, and closes a loophole that would have let funds appropriated elsewhere move to Border Patrol. That sentence is a compressed lesson in what appropriations negotiation actually is — not whether to fund, but which riders survive.

Now look up H.R. 6500 and you'll find the AGOA Extension Act — duty-free treatment for certain African imports. Not an error. The Constitution requires revenue bills to originate in the House, so the Senate takes an already-passed House bill, strips it to a shell, and drops the CR text in as a substitute. Everyone calls it the legislative vehicle. Ask the Cantwell staffer to explain it.

The four no votes are the interesting part

Kaine, Paul, Sanders and Warren. Two from the left, one libertarian right, one institutionalist. When a coalition fractures at both edges at once, the objection is almost always about process rather than content — governing by stopgap, no amendments allowed, take-it-or-leave-it timing.

Good question for Violet: why did those four end up in the same column as each other?

Cantwell's committee authorizes NASA — use this on Friday

Maria Cantwell is Ranking Member of Commerce, Science and Transportation — the committee you'll have sat in on Wednesday morning. This year it moved the NASA Authorization Act of 2026 and the Weather Act. The office that booked your Capitol tour holds the top Democratic seat on the committee that writes NASA's authorization, and you're at Air & Space the next morning.

Say so to the staffer. "We're at Air and Space tomorrow — what does the committee actually do for NASA?" hands Ryan a thread running from the Capitol to the Apollo capsule.

A bill possibly dying in front of you — the CLARITY Act

The crypto market-structure bill passed the House in 2025 and has been trying to reach the Senate floor ever since. It's now on a countdown to recess, still unscheduled, and three Democrats — Murphy, Van Hollen and Merkley — came out against it after a merged draft dropped an ethics provision they'd demanded.

This is the more useful lesson of the two. The CR shows a bill passing. CLARITY shows the far more common outcome: a bill with majority support dying from calendar time, one missing provision, and three senators. Most bills die.
